Kurt Vonnegut once said something simple and brilliant: **every story has a shape, and you can draw it.** Put time along the bottom of a chart, from the opening scene to the credits. Put "how well things are going for the hero" up the side — top is wonderful, bottom is awful. Now trace the line. A feel-good film rises. A tragedy slides down. A thriller plunges into a pit and crawls out.

We wondered: if a single film has a shape, does a whole *career* have one? So we took eight films by the Telugu director **Sukumar** — from his 2004 debut to 2024's *Pushpa 2* — and drew the line for each. Then we lined them up, oldest to newest, to see how his storytelling changed over twenty years.

The short answer: **his heroes grew up, and then they took over.**

## How we "drew" each film

We didn't watch the films by hand. We had a computer read each film's English subtitles from beginning to end — all the dialogue, in order — the way a careful viewer follows a story. As it read, it kept running notes (who the hero is, what they want, how it's going) and, at twenty points across the film, it rated thirteen simple things: how well the hero is doing, how tense the moment is, how much is at stake, whether the problem is getting solved, how much control the hero has, and so on.

The most important of those is **fortune** — plain old "is life going well for the hero right now." Chart it across the film and you get the story's shape. (The green line below is *resolution* — are things getting settled — and the pale line is *tension*.)

> One honest note up front: subtitles are *words*, not pictures. The computer hears what characters say, not the silent action, the fights, or the songs. And the scores are rough. So read these as a sketch of each film's shape, not a verdict on the film.

## Twenty years, one direction

Here's the whole career on one chart. Each dot is a film, in order of release. The gold line is **how much control the hero has by the end** — are they running things, or at the mercy of the world? The orange line is **whether the hero ends up better or worse than they started.**

![Sukumar's career in metrics](/img/sukumar/fig_career_timeline.png)

Both lines climb. In the early films the hero ends with little control and often worse off than they began. By the Pushpa films the hero ends fully in charge and far better off. Here are the heroes' fortune lines again, colored by era — the early films (green) tend to sag and fall; the recent films (red) rise and soar:

![Fortune by era](/img/sukumar/fig_fortune_by_era.png)

In plain terms: **Sukumar started out telling stories about underdogs who don't quite win — and grew into telling stories about outsiders who end up ruling everything.** The turning point is the Pushpa era.

**Shape:** Cinderella — up, a hard knock, then soaring. The purest climb of them all. Fortune goes from the floor to the ceiling, and the hero ends in total control. Rags to rule.


## What actually changed

Lining the eight films up by year, a few things move together (eight films is a small sample, so take these as the *direction* of travel, not hard science):

| Over the twenty years… | early films | recent films |
|---|---|---|
| Does the hero end in control? | barely (≈0.1) | almost fully (≈0.9) |
| Does the hero end better than they started? | usually worse | usually better |
| Do the problems get resolved by the end? | often left hanging | mostly settled |
| How tense / anxious is the telling? | higher | a little lower |

The clearest signal is **control**. Sukumar's early heroes are swept along — by love, by obsession, by fate. His recent heroes seize the wheel and don't let go. The stories also resolve more cleanly now: the early films can end on a question mark or a loss, while *Pushpa 2* ends about as high and settled as a story can.

## The fine print

This is one computer's reading of eight films, from subtitles, on a rough scale. It misses everything that isn't spoken — the visual grandeur, the action, the songs that carry so much of these films. One film, *Jagadam* (2007), isn't here at all, because we couldn't find English subtitles for it. And "a career has a shape" is a fun idea, not a theorem. But the trend is clear enough to feel true if you've watched these films: the man who once drew quiet defeats now draws triumphant rises.
